\textbf{Abstract---}Customer churn prediction is useful only when
predicted risk can be translated into a sensible retention decision.
Most telecom churn studies report accuracy, F1-score or ROC-AUC, yet the
operational consequences of a probability threshold are often less
visible. This study develops a leakage-safe framework that examines
class imbalance, probability thresholds, decision consequences and model
explanations within one experimental design. The IBM Telco Customer
Churn dataset, containing 7,043 customers, was evaluated with five
machine-learning algorithms and four imbalance-handling strategies.
Average Precision (AP) was used as the primary ranking metric under
stratified five-fold cross-validation. Tuned candidates were then
evaluated using leakage-free out-of-fold probabilities, threshold scans
and three transparent false-negative to false-positive scenarios (2:1,
5:1 and 10:1). A minimax relative-regret rule selected XGBoost trained
on the original data with a threshold of 0.18. On the untouched test
set, the model obtained AP 0.665 and ROC-AUC 0.847. Lowering the
threshold from 0.50 to 0.18 increased churn recall from 0.532 to 0.861
and reduced missed churners from 175 to 52, while false positives
increased from 97 to 380. The selected threshold reduced normalized
decision cost by 34.16\% and 51.27\% under the 5:1 and 10:1 scenarios,
respectively, but was 8.28\% worse under 2:1. SHAP analysis identified
Contract, tenure, InternetService, MonthlyCharges and OnlineSecurity as
the strongest model-level contributors. The results show that churn
models should be judged not only by ranking quality, but also by the
decisions created by their operating threshold.

\textbf{Keywords---}\emph{customer churn; class imbalance; decision
threshold; Average Precision; XGBoost; explainable AI}

\textbf{I. INTRODUCTION}

Customer churn is a persistent concern in telecommunications because
losing an established subscriber affects recurring revenue, service
planning and the cost of retention campaigns. Machine-learning methods
are now routinely used to estimate churn risk from customer
demographics, subscribed services, billing patterns and account history.
Recent studies have compared logistic regression, decision trees, random
forests, gradient boosting and neural models, while others have
introduced resampling, ensemble learning and explainable artificial
intelligence (Chang et al., 2024; Alotaibi and Haq, 2024; Asif et al.,
2025).

The central difficulty, however, is not simply to obtain a model with a
high score. Telecom churn data are commonly imbalanced, and the default
probability threshold of 0.50 can hide a large number of missed
churners. A model may rank customers well and still produce an operating
point that is poorly aligned with retention priorities. Manzoor et al.
(2024), after reviewing 212 churn studies, noted that
profitability-oriented evaluation remains under-researched and argued
for stronger links between predictive modelling and business decision
making. Poudel et al. (2024) similarly showed that reasonable overall
performance can coexist with a meaningful number of false negatives,
which is important when the objective is early retention action.

Class imbalance has been approached through class weighting, synthetic
oversampling and hybrid resampling. Ouf et al. (2024) and Wagh et al.
(2024), for example, investigated SMOTE-ENN-based strategies, whereas
Sikri et al. (2024) examined churn behaviour under different class
ratios. Explainability has also become more common through SHAP and LIME
(Chang et al., 2024; Asif et al., 2025). These developments are
valuable, but they are usually reported as separate modelling choices.
Within the reviewed literature, there is limited evidence of a single
leakage-safe evaluation process that connects imbalance handling,
threshold selection, false-negative/false-positive consequences and
post-hoc explanation.

This study addresses that gap by treating churn prediction as a decision
problem rather than a leaderboard problem. The work asks three
questions. RQ1 examines how imbalance strategies change model
performance under the same validation protocol. RQ2 studies how
probability thresholds alter the balance between detected churners and
unnecessary interventions, and identifies one model-threshold pair that
remains robust across several decision-cost assumptions. RQ3
investigates which customer attributes most strongly influence the final
model at both global and individual levels.

The contribution is therefore not a new classifier, resampling algorithm
or explainability method. Instead, the study integrates these components
into a retention-oriented framework: leakage-safe preprocessing,
controlled imbalance experiments, AP-based model ranking, out-of-fold
threshold analysis, transparent scenario costs, minimax relative-regret
selection and SHAP interpretation. The remainder of the paper reviews
related work, describes the methodology, reports the experimental
results, discusses their implications and closes with the main findings
and limitations.

\textbf{II. RELATED WORK}

Customer churn has been studied with a broad range of machine-learning
approaches. Manzoor et al. (2024) provide a comprehensive view of the
field, covering preprocessing, feature selection, modelling and
evaluation, and identify profit-aware evaluation and explainability as
important directions. In application studies, Chang et al. (2024)
combined telecom churn classification with SHAP and LIME, while Poudel
et al. (2024) used tabular machine-learning models and both global and
local SHAP explanations. These studies demonstrate that predictive
performance and interpretability can be considered together.

Imbalance handling is another recurring theme. Sikri et al. (2024)
analysed different class ratios and reported XGBoost results with
accuracy 89.60\%, precision 76.04\%, recall 62.82\% and F1-score 68.80\%
for one configuration. Ouf et al. (2024) integrated XGBoost with
SMOTE-ENN and reported large changes in classifier behaviour after
resampling. Wagh et al. (2024) also examined the IBM Telco data with
SMOTE-ENN and survival analysis. These findings make clear that
resampling can materially alter the minority-class operating point, but
they also underline the need for leakage-safe and comparable evaluation.

Several studies have focused on model comparison and optimisation. Saleh
and Saha (2023) compared multiple classifiers across telecom datasets,
including the IBM data. Alotaibi and Haq (2024) evaluated decision tree,
logistic regression, XGBoost, LightGBM, ANN and an averaging ensemble;
the ensemble did not automatically outperform the stronger individual
models. AlShourbaji et al. (2023) combined gradient boosting with
metaheuristic optimisation, oversampling and feature selection. More
recently, Asif et al. (2025) proposed an explainable ensemble of
XGBoost, CatBoost and LightGBM on a dataset exceeding two million
records and used SHAP and LIME to interpret predictions.

Taken together, the literature already contains strong work on
classifiers, ensembles, imbalance handling, tuning and XAI. The narrower
gap addressed here is the transition from probability ranking to
retention action: how training-time imbalance choices interact with
threshold selection, how many churners are missed or how many
non-churners are unnecessarily targeted, and how one operating point can
be selected when the exact relative cost of these errors is uncertain.

\textbf{III. METHODOLOGY}

The experimental design was organised so that every modelling choice was
made on development data and the final test set remained locked. Figure

final paper outputs. The main methodological stages are described below.

\emph{\textbf{A. Dataset and Data Preparation}}

The IBM Telco Customer Churn dataset contains 7,043 customers and 21
original columns. The target distribution is imbalanced: 5,174 customers
(73.46\%) are non-churners and 1,869 (26.54\%) are churners. The unique
customerID field was removed. TotalCharges, initially stored as an
object, contained 11 blank entries; these were converted to missing
numeric values and retained for fold-wise imputation. Churn was encoded
as 1 for Yes and 0 for No. No manual feature engineering was introduced.

Exploratory analysis showed clear descriptive differences between
churners and non-churners. Median tenure was approximately 10 months for
churners and 38 months for non-churners. Higher churn rates were
observed among month-to-month contracts, fibre-optic users,
electronic-check customers and customers without online security or
technical support. These patterns were treated as associations and were
not interpreted as causal effects.

\emph{\textbf{B. Locked Hold-out and Leakage-Safe Preprocessing}}

A stratified 80/20 split with random\_state = 42 created a development
set of 5,634 customers and a locked test set of 1,409. The development
set contained 4,139 non-churners and 1,495 churners; the test set
contained 1,035 and 374, respectively. All model comparison,
preprocessing, tuning and threshold selection used development data
only.

Three variables (tenure, MonthlyCharges and TotalCharges) were treated
as continuous and processed with median imputation followed by
standardisation. The remaining 16 predictors, including SeniorCitizen,
were treated as categorical or binary. They were imputed with the most
frequent category and ordinal-encoded before any sampling step. One-hot
encoding was applied after sampling. This ordering allowed SMOTENC to
operate on categorical-aware representations rather than interpolating
one-hot dummy variables. Every transformation was fitted inside the
training portion of each fold.

\emph{\textbf{C. Models, Imbalance Strategies and Validation}}

Five classifiers were evaluated: Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree
(DT), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost and LightGBM. Four imbalance
strategies were compared: Original data, Class Weight, SMOTENC and
SMOTENC followed by Edited Nearest Neighbours (ENN). In the hybrid
pipeline, ENN used n\_neighbors = 3 and sampling\_strategy = "all". The
design produced 20 baseline model-strategy combinations evaluated on the
same stratified five-fold splits.

Average Precision (AP) was the primary model-ranking metric because it
summarises precision-recall behaviour in an imbalanced binary problem.
ROC-AUC, precision, recall, F1-score, accuracy, false negatives (FN) and
false positives (FP) were retained as secondary measures. The AP leader
for each model was carried forward, together with selected near-best
strategies where AP loss was no greater than 0.005 and recall improved
materially. Seven candidates were then tuned by randomised search. In
total, 205 parameter configurations were evaluated over five folds
(1,025 fits).

\emph{\textbf{D. Out-of-Fold Probabilities and Threshold Analysis}}

Leakage-free out-of-fold (OOF) probabilities were generated for the
seven tuned candidates. Each development customer was predicted only by
a model that had not been trained on that customer. These OOF scores,
rather than the final test set, were used to study operating thresholds.

Thresholds from 0.01 to 0.99 were evaluated in steps of 0.01. For each
threshold, precision, recall, F1, FN and FP were recorded. The search
range was extended after an initial boundary check showed that one
high-FN-cost optimum occurred at the lower edge of the first scan. No
test information was used during this extension.

\emph{\textbf{E. Decision-Cost Framework}}

The dataset does not contain observed customer lifetime value,
retention-offer cost or profit margin. For that reason, the study does
not claim monetary profit. Instead, three transparent scenarios were
defined in which one missed churner was assumed to have two, five or ten
times the consequence of one unnecessary intervention. For threshold t
and relative FN cost r, normalized decision cost was defined as:

\[
C_r(t)=\frac{r \cdot FN(t)+FP(t)}{N}
\]

where N is the number of evaluated customers and r $\\in$ \{2, 5, 10\}. Lower
values indicate a more favourable decision under that assumed scenario.

\emph{\textbf{F. Robust Model-Threshold Selection}}

Different cost scenarios naturally favoured different thresholds. To
select one operating point without pretending that a single cost ratio
was known, relative regret was computed against the best achievable cost
within each scenario:

\[
R_{m,t,s}
=
\frac{
C_{m,t,s}-C_s^{*}
}{
C_s^{*}
}
\]

The final combination minimised the largest relative regret across the
three scenarios:

\[
(m^{*},t^{*})
=
\underset{m,t}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}
\;
\underset{s}{\max}
\;
R_{m,t,s}
\]

Ties were resolved using mean relative regret, mean normalized cost and
then OOF predictive quality. This procedure selected XGBoost trained on
the original distribution with threshold 0.18. The tuned model used 200
estimators, learning rate 0.05, max\_depth 2, min\_child\_weight 5,
subsample 0.8, colsample\_bytree 0.8, reg\_alpha 0 and reg\_lambda 1.0.

\emph{\textbf{G. Final Evaluation and SHAP}}

After the model and threshold were locked, the pipeline was refitted on
all development data and evaluated once on the untouched test set. SHAP
TreeExplainer was then applied to all 1,409 test customers. Encoded SHAP
values belonging to the same original feature were summed per customer
before computing mean absolute importance, preventing multi-level
categorical variables from being inflated simply because they produced
more dummy columns. SHAP was used only for explanation after model
selection and was not treated as evidence of causality.

\textbf{IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS}

\emph{\textbf{A. RQ1: Effect of Class-Imbalance Handling}}

The tuned results show that imbalance handling changed the operating
behaviour of the models more than it changed their ranking quality.
XGBoost on the original distribution achieved the highest mean CV AP
(0.6714), closely followed by class-weighted XGBoost (0.6700). The
class-weighted model, however, produced much higher recall at the
default threshold. LightGBM and Decision Tree with SMOTENC+ENN also
achieved high recall, but at lower AP and precision.

\emph{\textbf{B. RQ2: Threshold and Decision Consequences}}

The robust OOF selection chose XGBoost Original at threshold 0.18. Its
OOF AP and ROC-AUC were 0.6682 and 0.8496. At the selected threshold,
OOF recall reached 0.8823 with precision 0.4677 and F1 0.6114. The
maximum relative regret across the three cost scenarios was 0.1431,
lower than the competing model-threshold combinations.

On the locked test set, AP was 0.6650 and ROC-AUC was 0.8468. The small
changes from OOF (--0.0032 AP and --0.0028 ROC-AUC) indicate that ranking
performance transferred closely to unseen data. More importantly, the
operating threshold changed the error profile. At 0.50, recall was
0.5321 with 175 false negatives. At 0.18, recall rose to 0.8610 and
false negatives fell to 52. The cost of that gain was a rise in false
positives from 97 to 380 and a reduction in overall accuracy.

\emph{\textbf{C. RQ3: Explainability of the Final Model}}

The grouped SHAP analysis identified Contract as the most influential
original feature (mean \textbar SHAP\textbar{} = 0.6622), followed by
tenure (0.5118), InternetService (0.2908), MonthlyCharges (0.2298) and
OnlineSecurity (0.2215). PaymentMethod, TechSupport, PaperlessBilling,
MultipleLines and TotalCharges completed the top ten. At the encoded
level, month-to-month contract, tenure, fibre-optic internet,
MonthlyCharges, lack of OnlineSecurity, two-year contract, electronic
check and lack of TechSupport were among the strongest contributors.

Local explanations also showed why threshold choice matters for
individual customers. A high-confidence true positive received churn
probability 0.9187, while a borderline non-churner and one
false-negative churner received 0.1801 and 0.1795, respectively, placing
them on opposite sides of the selected decision boundary. SHAP therefore
helps explain how the model arrived at a risk score, but it does not
establish that changing one attribute would causally prevent churn.

\textbf{V. DISCUSSION}

Three observations stand out from the experiments. First, the choice of
imbalance strategy should not be judged at threshold 0.50 alone. Class
weighting substantially increased default-threshold recall, but the
corresponding AP remained almost unchanged relative to XGBoost trained
on the original data. Once thresholds were aligned to comparable recall,
the two XGBoost variants behaved almost identically. This suggests that
training-time imbalance handling and post-training threshold selection
are partly substitutable and should be analysed together rather than
reported as separate improvements.

Second, the final threshold exposes the practical cost of higher churn
sensitivity. At 0.18, the model detected 86.1\% of test churners,
compared with 53.2\% at 0.50. That gain reduced missed churners from 175
to 52, but it also generated 380 false positives. Under the 2:1 scenario
this was not worthwhile; under 5:1 and 10:1 it produced sizable cost
reductions. The result is therefore deliberately conditional. No
threshold is universally optimal. A retention team should choose an
operating point only after deciding how it values a missed churner
relative to an unnecessary intervention.

Third, the selected model remained interpretable at both portfolio and
customer level. Contract and tenure dominated the SHAP ranking, while
internet service, billing and support variables also contributed
strongly. These patterns are consistent with the broad direction
reported in explainable telecom churn studies (Chang et al., 2024;
Poudel et al., 2024), but the present analysis keeps a clear distinction
between model explanation and causality.

The study has several limitations. It uses one public dataset and
therefore does not establish external validity across operators or
countries. The decision-cost ratios are hypothetical because the data
contain no observed customer lifetime value, retention-offer cost,
campaign response or profit margin. Probability calibration was not
separately optimised, and the scores should be treated as risk scores
for ranking and thresholding rather than guaranteed frequencies.
External validation and prospective retention experiments were also
outside the scope of the work. Future studies could incorporate real
customer value and intervention costs, test calibrated probabilities,
validate on additional operators and compare a global threshold with
customer-specific treatment policies.

\textbf{VI. CONCLUSION}

This study presented a leakage-safe, imbalance-aware and threshold-aware
framework for telecom churn prediction. Five machine-learning models and
four imbalance strategies were compared, tuned and evaluated with AP as
the primary ranking measure. OOF probabilities were then used to study
thresholds and transparent FN:FP cost scenarios without touching the
final test set.

The final choice was XGBoost trained on the original distribution with
threshold 0.18. On the locked test set it achieved AP 0.6650 and ROC-AUC
0.8468. Relative to threshold 0.50, recall rose from 0.5321 to 0.8610
and false negatives fell from 175 to 52, although false positives
increased from 97 to 380. The selected operating point reduced
normalized cost under the 5:1 and 10:1 scenarios but not under 2:1. The
main conclusion is therefore straightforward: a strong churn model is
not enough; its threshold must be judged against the consequences of the
decisions it creates. SHAP further identified the customer attributes
most influential to the final model and provided a transparent layer for
interpreting individual risk scores.
